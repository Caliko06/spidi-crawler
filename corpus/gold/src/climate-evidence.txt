# Evidence

## Takeaways

- {3}While Earth’s climate has changed throughout its history, the current warming is happening at a rate not seen in the past 10,000 years.

- {3*}According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), "Since systematic scientific assessments began in the 1970s, the influence of human activity on the warming of the climate system has evolved from theory to established fact."1

- {2}Scientific information taken from natural sources (such as ice cores, rocks, and tree rings) and from modern equipment (like satellites and instruments) all show the signs of a changing climate.

- {2}From global temperature rise to melting ice sheets, the evidence of a warming planet abounds.

## The rate of change since the mid-20th century is unprecedented over millennia.

{1}Earth's climate has changed throughout history. {1}Just in the last 800,000 years, there have been eight cycles of ice ages and warmer periods, with the end of the last ice age about 11,700 years ago marking the beginning of the modern climate era — and of human civilization. {1}Most of these climate changes are attributed to very small variations in Earth’s orbit that change the amount of solar energy our planet receives.

{3*^}The current warming trend is different because it is clearly the result of human activities since the mid-1800s, and is proceeding at a rate not seen over many recent millennia. {2}It is undeniable that human activities have produced the atmospheric gases that have trapped more of the Sun’s energy in the Earth system. {2}This extra energy has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land, and widespread and rapid changes in the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, and biosphere have occurred.

{1}Earth-orbiting satellites and new technologies have helped scientists see the big picture, collecting many different types of information about our planet and its climate all over the world. {1}These data, collected over many years, reveal the signs and patterns of a changing climate.

{1}Scientists demonstrated the heat-trapping nature of carbon dioxide and other gases in the mid-19th century.2 Many of the science instruments NASA uses to study our climate focus on how these gases affect the movement of infrared radiation through the atmosphere. {2}From the measured impacts of increases in these gases, there is no question that increased greenhouse gas levels warm Earth in response.

{0}Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change

{2}Ice cores drawn from Greenland, Antarctica, and tropical mountain glaciers show that Earth’s climate responds to changes in greenhouse gas levels. {1}Ancient evidence can also be found in tree rings, ocean sediments, coral reefs, and layers of sedimentary rocks. {3*}This ancient, or paleoclimate, evidence reveals that current warming is occurring roughly 10 times faster than the average rate of warming after an ice age. {2}Carbon dioxide from human activities is increasing about 250 times faster than it did from natural sources after the last Ice Age.3

## The Evidence for Rapid Climate Change Is Compelling:

## Global Temperature Is Rising

- {3*}The planet's average surface temperature has risen about 2 degrees Fahrenheit (1 degrees Celsius) since the late 19th century, a change driven largely by increased carbon dioxide emissions into the atmosphere and other human activities. {1}Most of the warming occurred in the past 40 years, with the seven most recent years being the warmest. {1}The years 2016 and 2020 are tied for the warmest year on record.

- {0}Image credit: Ashwin Kumar, Creative Commons Attribution-Share Alike 2.0 Generic.

## The Ocean Is Getting Warmer

- {2}The ocean has absorbed much of this increased heat, with the top 100 meters (about 328 feet) of ocean showing warming of 0.67 degrees Fahrenheit (0.33 degrees Celsius) since 1969. {2}Earth stores 90% of the extra energy in the ocean.

- {0}Image credit: Kelsey Roberts/USGS

## The Ice Sheets Are Shrinking

- {2}The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have decreased in mass. {1}Data from NASA's Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment show Greenland lost an average of 279 billion tons of ice per year between 1993 and 2019, while Antarctica lost about 148 billion tons of ice per year.

- {0}Image: The Antarctic Peninsula, Credit: NASA

## Glaciers Are Retreating

- {2}Glaciers are retreating almost everywhere around the world — including in the Alps, Himalayas, Andes, Rockies, Alaska, and Africa.

- {0}Image: Miles Glacier, Alaska Image credit: NASA

## Snow Cover Is Decreasing

- {2}Satellite observations reveal that the amount of spring snow cover in the Northern Hemisphere has decreased over the past five decades and the snow is melting earlier.

- {0}Image credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech

## Sea Level Is Rising

- {2}Global sea level rose about 8 inches (20 centimeters) in the last century. {2}The rate in the last two decades, however, is nearly double that of the last century and accelerating slightly every year.

- {0}Image credit: U.S. {0}Army Corps of Engineers Norfolk District

## Arctic Sea Ice Is Declining

- {2}Both the extent and thickness of Arctic sea ice has declined rapidly over the last several decades.

- {0}Credit: NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio

## Extreme Events Are Increasing in Frequency

- {2}The number of record high temperature events in the United States has been increasing, while the number of record low temperature events has been decreasing, since 1950. {1}The U.S. has also witnessed increasing numbers of intense rainfall events.

- {0}Image credit: Régine Fabri, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

## Ocean Acidification Is Increasing

- {2}Since the beginning of the Industrial Revolution, the acidity of surface ocean waters has increased by about 30%. {1}This increase is due to humans emitting more carbon dioxide into the atmosphere and hence more being absorbed into the ocean. {1}The ocean has absorbed between 20% and 30% of total anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions in recent decades (7.2 to 10.8 billion metric tons per year).

- {0}Image credit: NOAA
