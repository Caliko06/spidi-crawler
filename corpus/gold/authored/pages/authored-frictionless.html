The Quiet Cost of Frictionless Things

I have been meaning to write this for a year, and I keep putting it off because it sounds like a complaint. My argument is that every time we remove a small difficulty from daily life we also remove a small piece of competence and a small occasion for meeting other people, and that the sum of these removals is larger than anyone has priced in. The argument is not against convenience as such. Let me begin with a story.

The bakery

For eleven years I bought bread from a bakery two streets from my flat, run by a woman named Marta who never remembered my name but always remembered my order. The queue was slow. Everything about the place was inefficient, because she weighed each loaf by hand, chatted with whoever was ahead of me and refused to take cards under five euros. When the bakery closed, a delivery app took over the street within a month.

The bread I get now arrives in forty minutes, costs less, and comes from a warehouse kitchen that bakes for six neighbourhoods at once. I will admit that it is good bread. What I lost was not bread but a fixed point in the week where I was recognised by someone who had no reason to be nice to me. It is hard to explain why that mattered.

Friction is where skill lives

Skills are built on friction: the resistance of a task is exactly what teaches us how to do it. A navigator who has never been lost does not learn to read a map, and a cook who has never burned a sauce does not learn the temperature of a pan. Researchers who study pilots have found that crews who rely most on automation are the slowest to recover when the automation fails. The same researchers note that the skills that decay first are the ones exercised least, which is why the decay is invisible until the day it matters.

The same pattern shows up in far humbler places, such as the way people who always use a navigation app stop forming mental maps of their own city. None of this is new, strictly speaking. Plato worried that writing would weaken memory, and he was partly right. The difference today is the speed and breadth of the removal: we are not outsourcing one skill at a time but dozens at once, and doing it by default rather than by choice.

Friction is also how we meet

Friction is also the medium of civic life, because shared queues, shared counters and shared mistakes are the places where strangers become neighbours. Sociologists call these places third spaces, and they have been closing for decades, long before the apps arrived. What the apps did was finish the job by making the visit unnecessary. A pharmacy that delivers does not need you to stand in its doorway, and a doorway is where you learn that the man ahead of you is caring for his mother.

Each of these encounters is trivial, and that is exactly why nobody defends them when they go. No one has ever campaigned to save a queue. Yet a city made only of efficient transactions is a city where nobody has a reason to speak to anyone.

The strongest objection

The obvious reply is that I am romanticising drudgery. People who carry shopping up four flights of stairs, or who wait three hours at a clinic, are not grateful for the character it builds, and for many of them frictionless services are a real liberation. I accept this completely, and it changes what I am asking for: I am not asking anyone to suffer, but to notice which difficulties are doing work. Disabled people, carers and the very poor have the strongest claim to convenience, and any defence of friction that ignores them is a defence of privilege.

There is a second objection that I find harder. It says that choosing friction deliberately is only a luxury hobby, like baking your own bread, and that a skill kept alive as a pastime is not the same thing as a skill a society depends on. I think this is mostly correct. A pastime keeps one person's hands in practice but it does not keep a shared counter open.

What to keep

The practical conclusion is to treat friction as a resource to be allocated, not as waste to be eliminated, and to ask of each new convenience what it quietly takes away. A useful test is to ask whether the difficulty teaches something that will be needed when the system fails, and whether it puts people in the same room. Where the answer to both is no, remove the difficulty gladly and without guilt. Where the answer is yes, a service can keep a deliberate, slower option, as some libraries keep a human desk beside the self-checkout.

Cities can protect a few counters and queues the way they protect a few parks, because their value is the same kind of value: nobody would build them from scratch for profit, and everybody notices when they are gone. I still do not know the name of the woman at the bakery. I think about her more often than I expected.
