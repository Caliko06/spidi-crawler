DOCUMENT: 20 Breakthroughs from 20 Years of Science aboard the International Space Station

§0 (under the title)
S0 Dive into 25 years of research aboard the space station here.
S1 For 20 years, the astronauts aboard the International Space Station have conducted science in a way that cannot be done anywhere else.
S2 Orbiting about 250 miles above our planet, the space station is the only laboratory available for long-duration microgravity research.
S3 During the past two decades, the space station has supported numerous discoveries, scientific publications, unique opportunities, and historic breakthroughs.
S4 This research not only helps us explore farther into space, it also benefits us back on Earth.
S5 To mark 20 years of science, take a look at 20 scientific and technological breakthroughs we have achieved as a result of space station science.
S6 Click on each breakthrough in the list below to learn more about it, why we conducted the research in microgravity, and why it matters.

§3 Discovery of steadily burning cool flames
S70 When we think about fire, we typically think about heat, but special flames created aboard the space station keep things a bit cooler.
S71 When scientists burned fuel droplets in the Flame Extinguishing Experiment (FLEX) study, something unexpected occurred.
S72 A fuel droplet appeared to extinguish but actually continued to burn without a visible flame.
S73 The fire went out twice—once with and once without a visible flame.
S74 This is the first time scientists observed large droplets of heptane fuel that had dual modes of combustion and extinction.
S75 The second stage was sustained by what is known as cool flame chemical heat release.
S76 Why do this in space?
S77 Removing gravity from studies of combustion allows for exploration of the basic principles of flames.
S78 Cool flames have been produced on Earth, but they quickly flicker out.
S79 On station, cool flames can burn for minutes, giving scientists a better opportunity to study them.
S80 Why does it matter?
S81 Typical flames produce soot, carbon dioxide, and water.
S82 Cool flames produce carbon monoxide and formaldehyde.
S83 Learning more about the behavior of these chemically different flames could lead to the development of more efficient, less-polluting vehicles.
S84 Example experiments: FLEX, FLEX-2, Cool Flames Investigation

§4 New water purification systems
S89 The space station’s life support system was developed to provide the crew with clean air and water.
S90 The Water Recovery System purifies and filters the station’s water, recovering and recycling 93% of the water astronauts use in space.
S91 This technology has been licensed to adapt it into an Earth-based water treatment system.
S92 The first of many ground-based water filtration systems using NASA technology was installed in Iraq in 2006.
S93 Station research studies like AquaMembrane have also tested out other innovative water systems.
S94 Why do this in space?
S95 Efficiently recycling wastewater aboard the space station reduces the need to provide water through resupply missions.
S96 As we travel deeper into space, resupply would be unachievable, making these systems a necessity.
S97 The restrictions imposed by the requirements of space prompted innovation that was applied to Earth.
S98 Why does it matter?
S99 Water is vital for human survival.
S100 Unfortunately, many people around the world lack access to clean water.
S101 At-risk areas can gain access to advanced filtration and purification systems through technology developed for the space station, making a lifesaving difference in these communities.

§5 Drug development using protein crystals
S109 Humans contain more than 100,000 types of proteins.
S110 Each protein provides information related to our health.
S111 Studying these proteins by crystallizing them helps us learn more about our bodies and potential disease treatments.
S112 Protein crystal growth experiments conducted aboard the space station have provided insights into numerous diseases, from cancer to gum disease.
S113 One of the most promising results of these station experiments has come from the study of a protein associated with Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD), an incurable genetic disorder.
S114 A treatment for DMD based on this research is in clinical trials.
S115 Another investigation, PCG-5, sought to grow the therapeutic antibody Keytruda® in a more uniform crystalline form.
S116 The goal was to improve the drug such that it can be delivered by injection as opposed to IV treatment.
S117 Why do this in space?
S118 Protein crystals grown on Earth are affected by gravity, which may alter the way the molecules align on the crystal.
S119 Researchers have discovered that growing crystals aboard the space station allows for slower growth and higher quality crystals.
S120 This high-quality crystallization allows us to identify the structures of disease-causing proteins to develop a new medications and effective treatments.
S121 Why does it matter?
S122 The space station is a tool that could be key in the fight against diseases the medical community has been working to combat for generations.
S123 DMD alone affects 1 in 3,600 young boys.

§6 Methods to combat muscle atrophy and bone loss
S130 The effect of microgravity on bones and muscles provides unique opportunities for research.
S131 Space studies have contributed greatly to our understanding of bone and muscle loss in astronauts, and in people on Earth.
S132 Scientists have developed an exercise routine and diet regimen that significantly reduce the bone and muscle loss astronauts otherwise would experience during their stays on station.
S133 These findings inform the physical activity and nutrition required on deep space missions.
S134 OsteoOmics investigated mechanisms behind bone formation and loss.
S135 Rodent Research-19 (RR-19) analyzed drugs that target myostatin, which influences the breakdown of muscles and bones.
S136 Researchers have even tested tiny chips for delivering drugs to combat muscle loss.
S137 Why do this in space?
S138 Our bodies evolved within the constant pull of Earth’s gravity.
S139 In microgravity, bones and muscles do not have to support the body’s mass.
S140 This can cause bone and muscle loss without proper exercise.
S141 This response to microgravity provides an opportunity for better understanding these changes.
S142 Why does it matter?
S143 Understanding how to mitigate the effects of microgravity on bones and muscles is important for future exploration in the partial gravity environments of the Moon and Mars.
S144 On Earth, bone and muscle atrophy occurs from normal aging, sedentary lifestyles, and illnesses.
S145 This condition may cause serious health issues from injuries due to falls or osteoporosis.
S146 Studying these losses in microgravity can help us better understand them and potentially create treatments for people back on Earth.
S147 Example experiments: RR-3, RR-6, RR-19, OsteoOmics, Medaka Osteoclast

§7 Exploring the 5th-state of matter
S154 25 years ago, scientists on Earth first produced a fifth state of matter with properties totally unlike solids, liquids, gases, and plasmas.
S155 The achievement garnered a Nobel Prize for those scientists and changed the field of physics.
S156 In 2018, NASA’s Cold Atom Lab became the first facility to produce that fifth state of matter, called a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC), in Earth’s orbit.
S157 The Cold Atom Lab lowers atoms to ultracold temperatures in order to study their properties in ways not possible on Earth.
S158 Why do this in space?
S159 Chilling atoms is the only way to produce a BEC.
S160 Ultracold atom facilities in space should be able to reach colder temperatures than labs on Earth.
S161 Additionally, scientists produce BECs in a vacuum, so on Earth the atoms are pulled down by gravity and fall quickly toward the floor.
S162 This movement typically limits observation times to less than a second without the help of magnetic or optical fields that influence the atoms’ behaviors.
S163 In microgravity, BEC’s can float, providing longer observation times.
S164 Why does it matter?
S165 BECs serve as a valuable tool for scientists studying quantum physics.
S166 BECs collectively exhibit properties typically displayed only by individual atoms, making those microscopic characteristics visible at a much larger scale.
S167 This study can provide insight into fundamental laws of quantum mechanics and could support the development of quantum technologies such as ultraprecise sensing and timekeeping.
S168 Example experiments: Cold Atom Lab – Bose-Einstein Condensate Bubble Dynamics, Cold Atom Lab – Few-Body Physics

§8 Understanding how our bodies change in microgravity
S173 Before humans head to Mars, we want to know what challenges we will face.
S174 Long-term stays aboard the space station have allowed us to uncover and begin solving challenges that could be presented by the human body’s response to space.
S175 For example, some space station astronauts unexpectedly developed vision changes, now known as Spaceflight-Associated Neuro-Ocular Syndrome (SANS).
S176 Findings include swelling at the optic disc and flattening at the back of the eyeball.
S177 After helping discover the issue, space station research has served as a platform to better understand SANS.
S178 NASA’s Twins Study compared astronaut Scott Kelly during his year in space with his Earth-bound twin brother Mark Kelly.
S179 It provided insights into the many ways long-term spaceflight affects a human body.
S180 Findings showed that Scott’s gene expression changed and his body reacted appropriately to vaccines while in space.
S181 Why do this in space?
S182 The space station currently is the only place for performing research in long-duration microgravity.
S183 There is no substitute for actually sending humans into orbit where they can serve as test subjects for the science.
S184 Why does it matter?
S185 Traveling deep into space means spending long periods of time in microgravity.
S186 To figure out how to make those trips safer and more comfortable, it is important to study humans closer to Earth in a controlled environment.
S187 Example experiments: Fluid Shifts, Twins Study, Vision Impairment and Intracranial Pressure, Direct ICP

§10 Testing tissue chips in space
S192 Tissue chips are roughly thumb-drive-sized devices that contain human cells in a 3D matrix, representing functions of an organ.
S193 They represent a big step in the ability of scientists to test how those cells respond to stresses, drugs, and genetic changes.
S194 The Tissue Chips in Space initiative seeks to use these devices in microgravity to better understand and improve human health and disease treatment on Earth.
S195 Chips simulating lung, kidney, brain, and intestine behavior all have been sent to the space station by a branch of the National Institutes of Health and the ISS U.S. National Lab.
S196 Why do this in space?
S197 Many of the changes in the human body caused by microgravity resemble the effects of diseases associated with aging on Earth, such as bone and muscle loss.
S198 In space, these changes occur much faster than they do on Earth.
S199 That means scientists may be able to use tissue chips in space to model changes that might take months or years to happen on Earth.
S200 Why does it matter?
S201 These studies can provide insights into diseases that affect organs on Earth and in space, and potentially help inform the creation of treatments for them.
S202 Example experiments: Gut on Chip, Organs-On-Chips as a Platform for Studying Effects of Microgravity on Human Physiology, Lung Host Defense in Microgravity, STaARS BioScience-7, Kidney Cells

§11 Stimulating the low-Earth orbit economy
S207 From satellite deployment to in-space research, a vibrant commercial space economy has developed.
S208 Through the ISS U.S. National Lab, many companies are able to access the space station and conduct research and technology development investigations in microgravity.
S209 They include well-known corporations like Procter & Gamble, which has used the station to study colloids with a goal of improving products such as shampoo and fabric softener.
S210 Smaller companies like LambdaVision have leveraged the microgravity environment on the space station to improve the processes used to create retinal implants.
S211 Supporting research activities on station is becoming a significant business too.
S212 Companies like Nanoracks and TechShot operate commercial research facilities housed on the station.
S213 A cadre of 40 companies help researchers translate their experiments into hardware small and light enough to be housed on station.
S214 Commercial resupply contracts to bring science and supplies to the space station also have helped support the development of launch services for companies such as Northrop Grumman and SpaceX.
S215 Why do this in space?
S216 Expanding use of the space station to commercial companies provides a new market, sparks economic development, and allows more groups to take advantage of this platform.
S217 Why does it matter?
S218 All of these commercial activities validate business models and expand the numbers of entities with experience in conducting business in space.
S219 Commercialization of low-Earth orbit frees up resources NASA needs to continue its exploration of space.
S220 By having companies join forces with government agencies, space exploration can continue, while providing access to a platform that helps create jobs, build revenues, and create new products and services for people on Earth.

§12 Growing food in microgravity
S228 As humans explore farther from Earth, the ability to grow food is a solution to several challenges of long-duration missions.
S229 Fresh produce provides nutritious meals over multiple years and reduces cargo requirements for the trip.
S230 Many techniques for growing plants have been explored on station to prepare for these missions.
S231 On August 10, 2015, astronauts sampled their first space-grown salad.
S232 Eight types of leafy greens have been grown in the Veggie facility for astronauts to eat, fine tuning the best techniques.
S233 Why do this in space?
S234 New solutions for watering, lighting, and growing plants have to be tested to create a food crop in microgravity.
S235 The space station has served as a platform for performing these tests, and for verifying what conditions allow the plants to grow most effectively.
S236 Why does it matter?
S237 The packaged diet used by crews in low-Earth orbit works well, but relies on frequent resupply missions.
S238 During a mission to Mars, the vitamins and quality of packaged food would degrade over time, and couldn’t easily be restocked.
S239 Supplementation with fresh crops provides necessary nutrients while giving astronauts more variety in their diet.

§13 Deployment of CubeSats from station
S247 CubeSats are one of the smallest types of satellites; they provide an inexpensive way to perform science and technology demonstrations in space.
S248 One way these compact cubes can reach Earth orbit is hitching a ride to the space station, where they are deployed from an airlock.
S249 Sharing the cost of a rocket launch with the other groups launching payloads to the space station can make deploying a CubeSat more affordable.
S250 Some of the first CubeSats released from the station were thrown by Russian cosmonauts while they performed a spacewalk.
S251 In 2012, crew members began using the Japanese Kibo module airlock to deploy CubeSats.
S252 Soon after, American company NanoRacks started operating a more robust deployer.
S253 One of NanoRacks’ first customers, Planet, deployed CubeSats to take high resolution photos of the entire Earth each day.
S254 Planet deployed several generations of CubeSats, demonstrating the viability of their technology and business model.
S255 NASA’s CubeSat Launch initiative provides a pathway to conduct research in space for CubeSats developed by educational institutions, non-profit organizations as well as NASA Centers and programs.
S256 Why do this in space?
S257 Putting these satellites into low-Earth orbit gives researchers and companies a unique perspective on our planet.
S258 More than 250 CubeSats have been deployed from station, performing numerous technology demonstrations, providing internet services, and more.
S259 Why it matters?
S260 Deploying CubeSats from the space station provides a more accessible way to get new technologies to space.
S261 Example experiments: NanoRacks-Planet Labs-Dove, AzTechSat-1, Phoenix CubeSat, NanoRacks-MinXSS

§14 Monitoring our planet from a unique perspective
S267 The view of Earth from the space station is life-changing for the few who get to see and photograph it in person.
S268 But this vantage point does more than create beautiful images: photos taken by astronauts can help track the evolution of our planet alongside the suite of tools inside and outside the space station.
S269 The orbiting lab has evolved into a robust platform for researchers studying Earth’s water, air, land masses, vegetation, and more.
S270 ECOSTRESS analyzes water stress in plants, while GEDI looks at the same areas of Earth, analyzing carbon stored in forests.
S271 Though the many experiments collect data individually, together they provide a set of measurements that pushes the leading edge of environmental research.
S272 Why do this in space?
S273 At about 250 miles above Earth on a 90-minute orbit and an orbital path over 90 percent of Earth’s population, the station affords a unique perspective that cannot be obtained on the ground, and it can provide improved spatial resolution and variable lighting conditions compared to the sun-synchronous orbits of typical Earth remote-sensing satellites.
S274 Why it matters: When scientists can better comprehend and monitor water and energy cycles, ecosystem changes, geological hazards, and population migrations, they can offer useful information regarding climate changes, as well as assist with natural disaster response.
S275 Example experiments: Crew Earth Observations, ECOSTRESS, GEDI, OCO-3, DESIS, HISUI

§15 Collecting data on more than 100 billion cosmic particles
S281 Many scientists theorize that stars, planets, and the molecules that comprise them are less than five percent of the mass-energy content of the universe.
S282 The rest is dark matter, invisible matter that cannot be detected directly but can be inferred.
S283 The Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer – 02 (AMS-02) has been looking for evidence of this substance from the space station since 2011, collecting data on more than 100 billion cosmic particles.
S284 AMS-02 has provided researchers around the globe with data that can help determine what the universe is made of and how it began.
S285 It also has collected data on cosmic rays, how these rays travel through space, and what produces them.
S286 Why do this in space?
S287 AMS-02’s core is a massive magnet that bends incoming charged particles.
S288 The direction they bend reveals their charge, which helps identify the type of particle.
S289 Earth’s atmosphere has a significant effect on these particles, so a space-based observatory is essential to get accurate information.
S290 Why it matters: In addition to helping unravel scientific mysteries of the universe, AMS-02 may also allow scientists to get a more complete picture of radiation in space.
S291 It is collecting data over a complete solar cycle, which provides more information about the potential radiation exposure for astronauts headed to Mars.

§16 A better understanding of pulsars and black holes
S297 Two tools installed on the outside of the space station have worked in tandem to advance our knowledge of pulsars and black holes: NASA’s Neutron star Interior Composition Explorer (NICER) and the Monitor of All-sky X-ray Image (MAXI) from the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA).
S298 In 2018, MAXI detected a new X-ray source in the sky and named it “MAXI J1820+070”.
S299 Shortly after, NICER began monitoring the source and determined it to be a black hole binary system containing a black hole with a mass several times that of our Sun.
S300 Within days, MAXI J1820+070 became one of the brightest X-ray sources in the sky.
S301 NICER tracked the evolution of this system.
S302 Its measurements helped us understand how the inner edge of a black hole’s accretion disk (and the corona above it) change in size and shape as a black hole consumes material from a star.
S303 Why do this in space?
S304 From above Earth, we can get a better look at the rest of the universe.
S305 Mounting multiple tools like NICER and MAXI on the same orbiting platform gives us more data on a large swath of the space around us.
S306 Why it matters: NICER and MAXI enable new studies of neutron stars and other astrophysical sources of X-rays, advancing scientific understanding and technical development for the benefit of people on Earth.

§18 Capability to identify unknown microbes in space
S331 Being able to identify microbes in space in real time – without having to send them back to Earth for identification – is revolutionary for the world of microbiology and space exploration.
S332 The Genes in Space-3 team proved this can be done when they completed the first-ever sample-to-sequence process entirely aboard the space station in 2017.
S333 Crew members collected a sample, isolated the DNA, prepped it, and then sequenced the unknown DNA.
S334 In the months leading up to this test, NASA astronaut Kate Rubins also conducted the first DNA sequencing in space.
S335 Why do this in space?
S336 Technology and scientific tools can behave differently in microgravity than on Earth.
S337 Designing DNA and microbial sequencers for use in space and testing these devices aboard the space station is crucial to ensuring their reliability on long-duration missions.
S338 Why it matters: A space-based DNA sequencer is an important tool to help protect astronaut health during long duration missions on the journey to Mars, aiding in the ability to diagnose and treat astronaut ailments in real time.
S339 Future explorers also could use the technology to identify DNA-based life forms beyond Earth.
S340 Example experiments: Genes in Space-3, Biomolecular Sequencer, Genes in Space-1

§19 Opening up the field of colloid research
S346 Toothpaste, 3D printing, pharmaceuticals, and detecting shifting sands on Mars may not seem related to each other at all, yet each stands to benefit from improvements made thanks to years of research on colloids aboard the space station.
S347 Mixtures of tiny particles suspended in a liquid, colloids occur in many forms.
S348 These forms include natural mixtures such as milk and muddy water as well as a range of manufactured products from shampoo to medicine to salad dressing.
S349 Space station research has explored subjects such as stabilizing these mixtures and colloid behaviors that can be applied to products on Earth.
S350 Why do this in space?
S351 Studying colloids on Earth is complicated by gravity, which causes some particles to rise and others to sink.
S352 Microgravity removes that complication and makes possible research that can help companies design better products.
S353 Why it matters: This colloid research can help to improve products used in our everyday lives and create entirely new products, including some that could enable exploration farther into space.
S354 Companies such as Procter & Gamble have used station research to study how to keep a product liquid enough to dispense easily and yet prevent ingredients, or particles, from clumping together and settling.
S355 Example experiments: ACE-H-2, ACE-T-6, ACE-T-10, BCAT, EXPPCS

§20 The evolution of fluid physics research
S360 Fluid covers our planet, but sending fluids to space can help us better understand them.
S361 Space station fluid research began with the fundamental analysis of how fluids behave in microgravity.
S362 That research has evolved into the testing of advanced medical devices and heat transfer systems.
S363 Rodent Research-6 tested tiny nanofluidic devices that, when implanted under the skin, could allow for the continuous delivery of drugs into a patient’s system.
S364 This process reduces the need for frequent injections or pills.
S365 A series of experiments used microgravity to study capillary forces, small forces that move a fluid along a narrow tube.
S366 Systems using capillary forces can be simpler because they rely on shapes and fluid dynamics rather than machinery to move liquid.
S367 Why do this in space?
S368 On Earth, gravity is one of the main factors that affects how fluid flows.
S369 However, there are additional smaller forces acting on fluids.
S370 Studying fluids in space, where gravity is not a factor, helps us to better understand these small forces and use them to our advantage.
S371 Why does it matter?
S372 An understanding of fluid physics in microgravity can influence everything from medical technologies and heat transfer systems on Earth to life support systems in space.
S373 Example experiments: Two-Phase Flow, Capillary Flow Experiment, Capillary Structures, Rodent Research-6, Dynamic Surf

§21 3D printing in microgravity
S379 The first 3D printer flew to the space station in 2014.
S380 Developed by Made in Space, the printer produced dozens of parts that researchers analyzed and compared with those made on the ground.
S381 Analysis revealed that microgravity had no significant effects on the process, demonstrating that a 3D printer works normally in space.
S382 Later experiments tested using recycled plastic to print objects and using a specialized bioprinter to print human tissue.
S383 The BioFabrication Facility (BFF) took small steps toward printing human organs and tissues in microgravity, using ultra-fine layers of bioink.
S384 Why do this in space?
S385 Testing printers on the station paves the way for future space missions to be more independent of Earth.
S386 Needed items could be 3D printed rather than sent from Earth and carried for the entire journey.
S387 Why does it matter?
S388 Using recycled material for printing could make use of material that otherwise would take up limited stowage space on long-duration missions and allow it to be used for creating needed parts.
S389 Printing human tissue is a part of a long-term plan to manufacture entire human organs in space using refined biological 3D printing techniques.
S390 This effort could help provide increased access to organs for transplants on Earth.
S391 Example experiments: Additive Manufacturing Facility, Made in Space Recycler, 3D Printing in Zero-G, AMF-ABS Design Values, Refabricator, BioFabrication Facility

§22 Responding to natural disasters
S397 With crew handheld camera imagery as a core component, the station has become an active participant in orbital data collection to support disaster response activities both within the U.S. and abroad.
S398 Astronauts snap images of disasters such as storms and fires throughout their progression, documenting cloud cover, flooding, and changes to the land.
S399 Pictures also are taken using mounted station cameras like the SS-HDTV and HDTV-EF2.
S400 SS-HDTV, developed to take night images of Earth, can be used to check if power has been restored to cities after a disaster.
S401 The Lightning Imaging Sensor (LIS) mounted on station also detects the distribution and variability of lightning in order to improve severe weather forecasting.
S402 The ISS SERVIR Environmental Research and Visualization System (ISERV) has helped developing countries enhance their environmental decision-making processes.
S403 Why do this in space?
S404 The space station has an inclined, low-Earth orbit that provides variable views and lighting over more than 90 percent of the inhabited surface of the Earth.
S405 This is a useful complement to sensor systems in higher-altitude polar orbits for collecting imagery.
S406 These pictures are referenced by teams on the ground.
S407 Why does it matter?
S408 These data allow for more informed responses to disasters from a perspective you cannot get on Earth, and assists with forecasting for future storms.
