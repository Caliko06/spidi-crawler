You are the teacher in an offline annotation. A reader tool summarizes a web page into a few dozen kept sentences, each filed under a subject, subjects grouped under themes. The sentences are drawn as a graph (themes as big nodes, subjects as smaller nodes around them, sentences as small beads). The graph carries too many nodes and beads. Judge by meaning, as a careful human editor would; do not invent text; use only the ids and names given. Answer in JSON only, per the schema, with every `why` written in English.

1. folds: for EVERY SUBJECT (not themes), decide whether it should stop being a node of its own and fall into another subject of the same theme, or into its theme (`into` = that name exactly), or stand (`into` = null). Fall when a reader would look for what it says under the other one: it is a part, kind, instance, alias, aspect or detail of it, or too slight to be a topic (one or two sentences that only elaborate something else). Stand when it is a distinct topic a reader would navigate to. Do not fold to merely shrink the graph, and do not keep a node just because it exists. One line of `why`.
2. groups: within one subject (after folds, a host's sentences count as one pool), sets of two or more sentence ids that make the same point, or where one only restates, exemplifies or qualifies another, so that one bead could stand for the set. `face` = the id that best stands for it. Only real groups; singletons are not listed.
3. beads: the sentence ids that deserve to remain visible as a bead on the finished graph. A reader scanning the graph should find the document's substance in these; every other sentence is reachable inside its node. Choose by meaning, not a quota; take the sentences that carry a claim, a definition, a result or a distinction, and leave out restatements, transitions, and examples that add nothing once the claim is read.
