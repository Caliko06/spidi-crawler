DOCUMENT: Do Things that Don't Scale


THEME "Experience"
  s0 What novice founders don't get is what insanely great translates to in a larval startup.
  s1 What founders have a hard time grasping (and Steve himself might have had a hard time grasping) is what insanely great morphs into as you roll the time slider back to the first couple months of a startup's life.
  s2 It's not the product that should be insanely great, but the experience of being your user.
  s3 The product is just one component of that.
  s4 [ESSENTIAL] But you can and should give users an insanely great experience with an early, incomplete, buggy product, if you make up the difference with attentiveness.
  s5 Over-engaging with early users is not just a permissible technique for getting growth rolling.
  s6 For most successful startups it's a necessary part of the feedback loop that makes the product good.
  s7 Even if you start the way most successful startups have, by building something you yourself need, the first thing you build is never quite right.
  s8 And except in domains with big penalties for making mistakes, it's often better not to aim for perfection initially.
  s9 In software, especially, it usually works best to get something in front of users as soon as it has a quantum of utility, and then see what they do with it.
  s10 Perfectionism is often an excuse for procrastination, and in any case your initial model of users is always inaccurate, even if you're one of them.
  s11 The feedback you get from engaging directly with your earliest users will be the best you ever get.

SUBJECT "Consult" (under theme "Experience")
  s12 Sometimes we advise founders of B2B startups to take over-engagement to an extreme, and to pick a single user and act as if they were consultants building something just for that one user.
  s13 The initial user serves as the form for your mold; keep tweaking till you fit their needs perfectly, and you'll usually find you've made something other users want too.
  s14 As long as you can find just one user who really needs something and can act on that need, you've got a toehold in making something people want, and that's as much as any startup needs initially.
  s15 So long as you're a product company that's merely being extra attentive to a customer, they're very grateful even if you don't solve all their problems.
  s16 But when they start paying you specifically for that attentiveness — when they start paying you by the hour — they expect you to do everything.
  s17 But in retrospect it was exactly the right thing to do, because it taught us how it would feel to merchants to use our software.

THEME "Delight"
  s18 [ESSENTIAL] You should take extraordinary measures not just to acquire users, but also to make them happy.
  s19 Your first users should feel that signing up with you was one of the best choices they ever made.
  s20 And you in turn should be racking your brains to think of new ways to delight them.
  s21 Another reason founders don't focus enough on individual customers is that they worry it won't scale.
  s22 But when founders of larval startups worry about this, I point out that in their current state they have nothing to lose.
  s23 Maybe if they go out of their way to make existing users super happy, they'll one day have too many to do so much for.
  s24 And incidentally, when it does, you'll find that delighting customers scales better than you expected.
  s25 I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy.
  s26 But perhaps the biggest thing preventing founders from realizing how attentive they could be to their users is that they've never experienced such attention themselves.
  s27 But you can.
  s28 That's one advantage of being small: you can provide a level of service no big company can.

THEME "Recruit"
  s29 [ESSENTIAL] The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually.
  s30 [ESSENTIAL] You can't wait for users to come to you.
  s31 You have to go out and get them.
  s32 But for a startup to succeed, at least one founder (usually the CEO) will have to spend a lot of time on sales and marketing.
  s33 The mistake they make is to underestimate the power of compound growth.
  s34 If you have 100 users, you need to get 10 more next week to grow 10% a week.
  s35 And while 110 may not seem much better than 100, if you keep growing at 10% a week you'll be surprised how big the numbers get.
  s36 But if the market exists you can usually start by recruiting users manually and then gradually switch to less manual methods.

SUBJECT "Big" (under theme "Recruit")
  s37 All you need from a launch is some initial core of users.
  s38 How well you're doing a few months later will depend more on how happy you made those users than how many there were of them.
  s39 But even if what you're building really is great, getting users will always be a gradual process — partly because great things are usually also novel, but mainly because users have other things to think about.
  s40 Partnerships too usually don't work.
  s41 They don't work for startups in general, but they especially don't work as a way to get growth started.
  s42 It's not enough just to do something extraordinary initially.
  s43 [ESSENTIAL] You have to make an extraordinary effort initially.
  s44 Any strategy that omits the effort — whether it's expecting a big launch to get you users, or a big partner — is ipso facto suspect.

SUBJECT "Fire" (under theme "Recruit")
  s45 Sometimes the right unscalable trick is to focus on a deliberately narrow market.
  s46 Any startup that could be described as a marketplace usually has to start in a subset of the market, but this can work for other startups as well.
  s47 It's always worth asking if there's a subset of the market in which you can get a critical mass of users quickly.
  s48 Most startups that use the contained fire strategy do it unconsciously.
  s49 The biggest danger of not being consciously aware of this pattern is for those who naively discard part of it.

THEME "Vector"
  s50 [ESSENTIAL] The need to do something unscalably laborious to get started is so nearly universal that it might be a good idea to stop thinking of startup ideas as scalars.
  s51 Instead we should try thinking of them as pairs of what you're going to build, plus the unscalable thing(s) you're going to do initially to get the company going.
  s52 It could be interesting to start viewing startup ideas this way, because now that there are two components you can try to be imaginative about the second as well as the first.
  s53 [ESSENTIAL] But in most cases the second component will be what it usually is — recruit users manually and give them an overwhelmingly good experience — and the main benefit of treating startups as vectors will be to remind founders they need to work hard in two dimensions.
  s54 In the best case, both components of the vector contribute to your company's DNA: the unscalable things you have to do to get started are not merely a necessary evil, but change the company permanently for the better.
  s55 If you have to be aggressive about user acquisition when you're small, you'll probably still be aggressive when you're big.
  s56 And most importantly, if you have to work hard to delight users when you only have a handful of them, you'll keep doing it when you have a lot.

THEME "Fragile"
  s57 There may be a handful that just grew by themselves, but usually it takes some sort of push to get them going.
  s58 Almost all startups are fragile initially.
  s59 And that's one of the biggest things inexperienced founders and investors (and reporters and know-it-alls on forums) get wrong about them.
  s60 They unconsciously judge larval startups by the standards of established ones.
  s61 The big danger is that you'll dismiss your startup yourself.
  s62 The question to ask about an early stage startup is not "is this company taking over the world?" but "how big could this company get if the founders did the right things?" And the right things often seem both laborious and inconsequential at the time.
  s63 The usual way to do that is to get some initial set of users by doing a comparatively untargeted launch, and then to observe which kind seem most enthusiastic, and seek out more like them.

THEME "Do Things that Don't Scale"
  s64 [LEAD] One of the most common types of advice we give at Y Combinator is to do things that don't scale.
  s65 [ESSENTIAL] Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off.

SUBJECT "Manual" (under theme "Do Things that Don't Scale")
  s66 When you only have a small number of users, you can sometimes get away with doing by hand things that you plan to automate later.
  s67 This lets you launch faster, and when you do finally automate yourself out of the loop, you'll know exactly what to build because you'll have muscle memory from doing it yourself.
  s68 If you can find someone with a problem that needs solving and you can solve it manually, go ahead and do that for as long as you can, and then gradually automate the bottlenecks.
  s69 It would be a little frightening to be solving users' problems in a way that wasn't yet automatic, but less frightening than the far more common case of having something automatic that doesn't yet solve anyone's problems.

SUBJECT "Meraki" (under theme "Do Things that Don't Scale")
  s70 But even so I'd advise startups to pull a Meraki initially if they can.
  s71 Like paying excessive attention to early customers, fabricating things yourself turns out to be valuable for hardware startups.
  s72 You can tweak the design faster when you're the factory, and you learn things you'd never have known otherwise.
