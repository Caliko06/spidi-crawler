DOCUMENT: Startup = Growth

§0 (under the title)
S0 September 2012
S1 A startup is a company designed to grow fast.
S2 Being newly founded does not in itself make a company a startup.
S3 Nor is it necessary for a startup to work on technology, or take venture funding, or have some sort of "exit." The only essential thing is growth.
S4 Everything else we associate with startups follows from growth.
S5 If you want to start one it's important to understand that.
S6 Startups are so hard that you can't be pointed off to the side and hope to succeed.
S7 You have to know that growth is what you're after.
S8 The good news is, if you get growth, everything else tends to fall into place.
S9 Which means you can use growth like a compass to make almost every decision you face.

§1 Redwoods
S10 Let's start with a distinction that should be obvious but is often overlooked: not every newly founded company is a startup.
S11 Millions of companies are started every year in the US.
S12 Only a tiny fraction are startups.
S13 Most are service businesses — restaurants, barbershops, plumbers, and so on.
S14 These are not startups, except in a few unusual cases.
S15 A barbershop isn't designed to grow fast.
S16 Whereas a search engine, for example, is.
S17 When I say startups are designed to grow fast, I mean it in two senses.
S18 Partly I mean designed in the sense of intended, because most startups fail.
S19 But I also mean startups are different by nature, in the same way a redwood seedling has a different destiny from a bean sprout.
S20 That difference is why there's a distinct word, "startup," for companies designed to grow fast.
S21 If all companies were essentially similar, but some through luck or the efforts of their founders ended up growing very fast, we wouldn't need a separate word.
S22 We could just talk about super-successful companies and less successful ones.
S23 But in fact startups do have a different sort of DNA from other businesses.
S24 Google is not just a barbershop whose founders were unusually lucky and hard-working.
S25 Google was different from the beginning.
S26 To grow rapidly, you need to make something you can sell to a big market.
S27 That's the difference between Google and a barbershop.
S28 A barbershop doesn't scale.
S29 For a company to grow really big, it must (a) make something lots of people want, and (b) reach and serve all those people.
S30 Barbershops are doing fine in the (a) department.
S31 Almost everyone needs their hair cut.
S32 The problem for a barbershop, as for any retail establishment, is (b).
S33 A barbershop serves customers in person, and few will travel far for a haircut.
S34 And even if they did, the barbershop couldn't accommodate them.
S35 Writing software is a great way to solve (b), but you can still end up constrained in (a).
S36 If you write software to teach Tibetan to Hungarian speakers, you'll be able to reach most of the people who want it, but there won't be many of them.
S37 If you make software to teach English to Chinese speakers, however, you're in startup territory.
S38 Most businesses are tightly constrained in (a) or (b).
S39 The distinctive feature of successful startups is that they're not.

§2 Ideas
S40 It might seem that it would always be better to start a startup than an ordinary business.
S41 If you're going to start a company, why not start the type with the most potential?
S42 The catch is that this is a (fairly) efficient market.
S43 If you write software to teach Tibetan to Hungarians, you won't have much competition.
S44 If you write software to teach English to Chinese speakers, you'll face ferocious competition, precisely because that's such a larger prize.
S45 The constraints that limit ordinary companies also protect them.
S46 That's the tradeoff.
S47 If you start a barbershop, you only have to compete with other local barbers.
S48 If you start a search engine you have to compete with the whole world.
S49 The most important thing that the constraints on a normal business protect it from is not competition, however, but the difficulty of coming up with new ideas.
S50 If you open a bar in a particular neighborhood, as well as limiting your potential and protecting you from competitors, that geographic constraint also helps define your company.
S51 Bar + neighborhood is a sufficient idea for a small business.
S52 Similarly for companies constrained in (a).
S53 Your niche both protects and defines you.
S54 Whereas if you want to start a startup, you're probably going to have to think of something fairly novel.
S55 A startup has to make something it can deliver to a large market, and ideas of that type are so valuable that all the obvious ones are already taken.
S56 That space of ideas has been so thoroughly picked over that a startup generally has to work on something everyone else has overlooked.
S57 I was going to write that one has to make a conscious effort to find ideas everyone else has overlooked.
S58 But that's not how most startups get started.
S59 Usually successful startups happen because the founders are sufficiently different from other people that ideas few others can see seem obvious to them.
S60 Perhaps later they step back and notice they've found an idea in everyone else's blind spot, and from that point make a deliberate effort to stay there.
S61 [3] But at the moment when successful startups get started, much of the innovation is unconscious.
S62 What's different about successful founders is that they can see different problems.
S63 It's a particularly good combination both to be good at technology and to face problems that can be solved by it, because technology changes so rapidly that formerly bad ideas often become good without anyone noticing.
S64 Steve Wozniak's problem was that he wanted his own computer.
S65 That was an unusual problem to have in 1975.
S66 But technological change was about to make it a much more common one.
S67 Because he not only wanted a computer but knew how to build them, Wozniak was able to make himself one.
S68 And the problem he solved for himself became one that Apple solved for millions of people in the coming years.
S69 But by the time it was obvious to ordinary people that this was a big market, Apple was already established.
S70 Google has similar origins.
S71 Larry Page and Sergey Brin wanted to search the web.
S72 But unlike most people they had the technical expertise both to notice that existing search engines were not as good as they could be, and to know how to improve them.
S73 Over the next few years their problem became everyone's problem, as the web grew to a size where you didn't have to be a picky search expert to notice the old algorithms weren't good enough.
S74 But as happened with Apple, by the time everyone else realized how important search was, Google was entrenched.
S75 That's one connection between startup ideas and technology.
S76 Rapid change in one area uncovers big, soluble problems in other areas.
S77 Sometimes the changes are advances, and what they change is solubility.
S78 That was the kind of change that yielded Apple; advances in chip technology finally let Steve Wozniak design a computer he could afford.
S79 But in Google's case the most important change was the growth of the web.
S80 What changed there was not solubility but bigness.
S81 The other connection between startups and technology is that startups create new ways of doing things, and new ways of doing things are, in the broader sense of the word, new technology.
S82 When a startup both begins with an idea exposed by technological change and makes a product consisting of technology in the narrower sense (what used to be called "high technology"), it's easy to conflate the two.
S83 But the two connections are distinct and in principle one could start a startup that was neither driven by technological change, nor whose product consisted of technology except in the broader sense.

§3 Rate
S84 How fast does a company have to grow to be considered a startup?
S85 There's no precise answer to that.
S86 "Startup" is a pole, not a threshold.
S87 Starting one is at first no more than a declaration of one's ambitions.
S88 You're committing not just to starting a company, but to starting a fast growing one, and you're thus committing to search for one of the rare ideas of that type.
S89 But at first you have no more than commitment.
S90 Starting a startup is like being an actor in that respect.
S91 "Actor" too is a pole rather than a threshold.
S92 At the beginning of his career, an actor is a waiter who goes to auditions.
S93 Getting work makes him a successful actor, but he doesn't only become an actor when he's successful.
S94 So the real question is not what growth rate makes a company a startup, but what growth rate successful startups tend to have.
S95 For founders that's more than a theoretical question, because it's equivalent to asking if they're on the right path.
S96 The growth of a successful startup usually has three phases:
S97 There's an initial period of slow or no growth while the startup tries to figure out what it's doing.
S98 As the startup figures out how to make something lots of people want and how to reach those people, there's a period of rapid growth.
S99 Eventually a successful startup will grow into a big company.
S100 Growth will slow, partly due to internal limits and partly because the company is starting to bump up against the limits of the markets it serves.
S101 The slope is the company's growth rate.
S102 If there's one number every founder should always know, it's the company's growth rate.
S103 That's the measure of a startup.
S104 If you don't know that number, you don't even know if you're doing well or badly.
S105 When I first meet founders and ask what their growth rate is, sometimes they tell me "we get about a hundred new customers a month." That's not a rate.
S106 What matters is not the absolute number of new customers, but the ratio of new customers to existing ones.
S107 If you're really getting a constant number of new customers every month, you're in trouble, because that means your growth rate is decreasing.
S108 During Y Combinator we measure growth rate per week, partly because there is so little time before Demo Day, and partly because startups early on need frequent feedback from their users to tweak what they're doing.
S109 A good growth rate during YC is 5-7% a week.
S110 If you can hit 10% a week you're doing exceptionally well.
S111 If you can only manage 1%, it's a sign you haven't yet figured out what you're doing.
S112 The best thing to measure the growth rate of is revenue.
S113 The next best, for startups that aren't charging initially, is active users.
S114 That's a reasonable proxy for revenue growth because whenever the startup does start trying to make money, their revenues will probably be a constant multiple of active users.

§4 Compass
S115 We usually advise startups to pick a growth rate they think they can hit, and then just try to hit it every week.
S116 The key word here is "just." If they decide to grow at 7% a week and they hit that number, they're successful for that week.
S117 There's nothing more they need to do.
S118 But if they don't hit it, they've failed in the only thing that mattered, and should be correspondingly alarmed.
S119 Programmers will recognize what we're doing here.
S120 We're turning starting a startup into an optimization problem.
S121 And anyone who has tried optimizing code knows how wonderfully effective that sort of narrow focus can be.
S122 Optimizing code means taking an existing program and changing it to use less of something, usually time or memory.
S123 You don't have to think about what the program should do, just make it faster.
S124 For most programmers this is very satisfying work.
S125 The narrow focus makes it a sort of puzzle, and you're generally surprised how fast you can solve it.
S126 Focusing on hitting a growth rate reduces the otherwise bewilderingly multifarious problem of starting a startup to a single problem.
S127 You can use that target growth rate to make all your decisions for you; anything that gets you the growth you need is ipso facto right.
S128 Should you spend two days at a conference?
S129 Should you hire another programmer?
S130 Should you focus more on marketing?
S131 Should you spend time courting some big customer?
S132 Should you add x feature?
S133 Whatever gets you your target growth rate.
S134 Judging yourself by weekly growth doesn't mean you can look no more than a week ahead.
S135 Once you experience the pain of missing your target one week (it was the only thing that mattered, and you failed at it), you become interested in anything that could spare you such pain in the future.
S136 So you'll be willing for example to hire another programmer, who won't contribute to this week's growth but perhaps in a month will have implemented some new feature that will get you more users.
S137 But only if (a) the distraction of hiring someone won't make you miss your numbers in the short term, and (b) you're sufficiently worried about whether you can keep hitting your numbers without hiring someone new.
S138 It's not that you don't think about the future, just that you think about it no more than necessary.
S139 In theory this sort of hill-climbing could get a startup into trouble.
S140 They could end up on a local maximum.
S141 But in practice that never happens.
S142 Having to hit a growth number every week forces founders to act, and acting versus not acting is the high bit of succeeding.
S143 Nine times out of ten, sitting around strategizing is just a form of procrastination.
S144 Whereas founders' intuitions about which hill to climb are usually better than they realize.
S145 Plus the maxima in the space of startup ideas are not spiky and isolated.
S146 Most fairly good ideas are adjacent to even better ones.
S147 The fascinating thing about optimizing for growth is that it can actually discover startup ideas.
S148 You can use the need for growth as a form of evolutionary pressure.
S149 If you start out with some initial plan and modify it as necessary to keep hitting, say, 10% weekly growth, you may end up with a quite different company than you meant to start.
S150 But anything that grows consistently at 10% a week is almost certainly a better idea than you started with.
S151 There's a parallel here to small businesses.
S152 Just as the constraint of being located in a particular neighborhood helps define a bar, the constraint of growing at a certain rate can help define a startup.
S153 You'll generally do best to follow that constraint wherever it leads rather than being influenced by some initial vision, just as a scientist is better off following the truth wherever it leads rather than being influenced by what he wishes were the case.
S154 When Richard Feynman said that the imagination of nature was greater than the imagination of man, he meant that if you just keep following the truth you'll discover cooler things than you could ever have made up.
S155 For startups, growth is a constraint much like truth.
S156 Every successful startup is at least partly a product of the imagination of growth.

§5 Value
S157 It's hard to find something that grows consistently at several percent a week, but if you do you may have found something surprisingly valuable.
S158 If we project forward we see why.
S159 A company that grows at 1% a week will grow 1.7x a year, whereas a company that grows at 5% a week will grow 12.6x.
S160 A company making $1000 a month (a typical number early in YC) and growing at 1% a week will 4 years later be making $7900 a month, which is less than a good programmer makes in salary in Silicon Valley.
S161 A startup that grows at 5% a week will in 4 years be making $25 million a month.
S162 Our ancestors must rarely have encountered cases of exponential growth, because our intuitions are no guide here.
S163 What happens to fast growing startups tends to surprise even the founders.
S164 Small variations in growth rate produce qualitatively different outcomes.
S165 That's why there's a separate word for startups, and why startups do things that ordinary companies don't, like raising money and getting acquired.
S166 And, strangely enough, it's also why they fail so frequently.
S167 Considering how valuable a successful startup can become, anyone familiar with the concept of expected value would be surprised if the failure rate weren't high.
S168 If a successful startup could make a founder $100 million, then even if the chance of succeeding were only 1%, the expected value of starting one would be $1 million.
S169 And the probability of a group of sufficiently smart and determined founders succeeding on that scale might be significantly over 1%.
S170 For the right people — e.g. the young Bill Gates — the probability might be 20% or even 50%.
S171 So it's not surprising that so many want to take a shot at it.
S172 In an efficient market, the number of failed startups should be proportionate to the size of the successes.
S173 And since the latter is huge the former should be too.
S174 What this means is that at any given time, the great majority of startups will be working on something that's never going to go anywhere, and yet glorifying their doomed efforts with the grandiose title of "startup."
S175 This doesn't bother me.
S176 It's the same with other high-beta vocations, like being an actor or a novelist.
S177 I've long since gotten used to it.
S178 But it seems to bother a lot of people, particularly those who've started ordinary businesses.
S179 Many are annoyed that these so-called startups get all the attention, when hardly any of them will amount to anything.
S180 If they stepped back and looked at the whole picture they might be less indignant.
S181 The mistake they're making is that by basing their opinions on anecdotal evidence they're implicitly judging by the median rather than the average.
S182 If you judge by the median startup, the whole concept of a startup seems like a fraud.
S183 You have to invent a bubble to explain why founders want to start them or investors want to fund them.
S184 But it's a mistake to use the median in a domain with so much variation.
S185 If you look at the average outcome rather than the median, you can understand why investors like them, and why, if they aren't median people, it's a rational choice for founders to start them.

§6 Deals
S186 Why do investors like startups so much?
S187 Why are they so hot to invest in photo-sharing apps, rather than solid money-making businesses?
S188 Not only for the obvious reason.
S189 The test of any investment is the ratio of return to risk.
S190 Startups pass that test because although they're appallingly risky, the returns when they do succeed are so high.
S191 But that's not the only reason investors like startups.
S192 An ordinary slower-growing business might have just as good a ratio of return to risk, if both were lower.
S193 So why are VCs interested only in high-growth companies?
S194 The reason is that they get paid by getting their capital back, ideally after the startup IPOs, or failing that when it's acquired.
S195 The other way to get returns from an investment is in the form of dividends.
S196 Why isn't there a parallel VC industry that invests in ordinary companies in return for a percentage of their profits?
S197 Because it's too easy for people who control a private company to funnel its revenues to themselves (e.g. by buying overpriced components from a supplier they control) while making it look like the company is making little profit.
S198 Anyone who invested in private companies in return for dividends would have to pay close attention to their books.
S199 The reason VCs like to invest in startups is not simply the returns, but also because such investments are so easy to oversee.
S200 The founders can't enrich themselves without also enriching the investors.
S201 Why do founders want to take the VCs' money?
S202 Growth, again.
S203 The constraint between good ideas and growth operates in both directions.
S204 It's not merely that you need a scalable idea to grow.
S205 If you have such an idea and don't grow fast enough, competitors will.
S206 Growing too slowly is particularly dangerous in a business with network effects, which the best startups usually have to some degree.
S207 Almost every company needs some amount of funding to get started.
S208 But startups often raise money even when they are or could be profitable.
S209 It might seem foolish to sell stock in a profitable company for less than you think it will later be worth, but it's no more foolish than buying insurance.
S210 Fundamentally that's how the most successful startups view fundraising.
S211 They could grow the company on its own revenues, but the extra money and help supplied by VCs will let them grow even faster.
S212 Raising money lets you choose your growth rate.
S213 Money to grow faster is always at the command of the most successful startups, because the VCs need them more than they need the VCs.
S214 A profitable startup could if it wanted just grow on its own revenues.
S215 Growing slower might be slightly dangerous, but chances are it wouldn't kill them.
S216 Whereas VCs need to invest in startups, and in particular the most successful startups, or they'll be out of business.
S217 Which means that any sufficiently promising startup will be offered money on terms they'd be crazy to refuse.
S218 And yet because of the scale of the successes in the startup business, VCs can still make money from such investments.
S219 You'd have to be crazy to believe your company was going to become as valuable as a high growth rate can make it, but some do.
S220 Pretty much every successful startup will get acquisition offers too.
S221 Why?
S222 What is it about startups that makes other companies want to buy them?
S223 Fundamentally the same thing that makes everyone else want the stock of successful startups: a rapidly growing company is valuable.
S224 It's a good thing eBay bought Paypal, for example, because Paypal is now responsible for 43% of their sales and probably more of their growth.
S225 But acquirers have an additional reason to want startups.
S226 A rapidly growing company is not merely valuable, but dangerous.
S227 If it keeps expanding, it might expand into the acquirer's own territory.
S228 Most product acquisitions have some component of fear.
S229 Even if an acquirer isn't threatened by the startup itself, they might be alarmed at the thought of what a competitor could do with it.
S230 And because startups are in this sense doubly valuable to acquirers, acquirers will often pay more than an ordinary investor would.

§7 Understand
S231 The combination of founders, investors, and acquirers forms a natural ecosystem.
S232 It works so well that those who don't understand it are driven to invent conspiracy theories to explain how neatly things sometimes turn out.
S233 Just as our ancestors did to explain the apparently too neat workings of the natural world.
S234 But there is no secret cabal making it all work.
S235 If you start from the mistaken assumption that Instagram was worthless, you have to invent a secret boss to force Mark Zuckerberg to buy it.
S236 To anyone who knows Mark Zuckerberg, that is the reductio ad absurdum of the initial assumption.
S237 The reason he bought Instagram was that it was valuable and dangerous, and what made it so was growth.
S238 If you want to understand startups, understand growth.
S239 Growth drives everything in this world.
S240 Growth is why startups usually work on technology — because ideas for fast growing companies are so rare that the best way to find new ones is to discover those recently made viable by change, and technology is the best source of rapid change.
S241 Growth is why it's a rational choice economically for so many founders to try starting a startup: growth makes the successful companies so valuable that the expected value is high even though the risk is too.
S242 Growth is why VCs want to invest in startups: not just because the returns are high but also because generating returns from capital gains is easier to manage than generating returns from dividends.
S243 Growth explains why the most successful startups take VC money even if they don't need to: it lets them choose their growth rate.
S244 And growth explains why successful startups almost invariably get acquisition offers.
S245 To acquirers a fast-growing company is not merely valuable but dangerous too.
S246 It's not just that if you want to succeed in some domain, you have to understand the forces driving it.
S247 Understanding growth is what starting a startup consists of.
S248 What you're really doing (and to the dismay of some observers, all you're really doing) when you start a startup is committing to solve a harder type of problem than ordinary businesses do.
S249 You're committing to search for one of the rare ideas that generates rapid growth.
S250 Because these ideas are so valuable, finding one is hard.
S251 The startup is the embodiment of your discoveries so far.
S252 Starting a startup is thus very much like deciding to be a research scientist: you're not committing to solve any specific problem; you don't know for sure which problems are soluble; but you're committing to try to discover something no one knew before.
S253 A startup founder is in effect an economic research scientist.
S254 Most don't discover anything that remarkable, but some discover relativity.
