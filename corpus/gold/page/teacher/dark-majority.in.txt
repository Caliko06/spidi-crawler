DOCUMENT: The Dark Majority

§0 (under the title)
S0 Most of the living space on this planet has never seen sunlight.
S1 We have barely looked at it.

§1 A planet of deep water
S2 Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface, but the surface is the smallest part of the story.
S3 The average depth is close to 3,700 metres.
S4 Sunlight gives up long before that.
S5 By 200 metres there is too little of it for photosynthesis, and by 1,000 metres it is gone completely.
S6 Everything below that line is the deep sea.
S7 It holds roughly 90% of the ocean's volume.
S8 In other words, the typical place to live on Earth is cold, dark and pressed under kilometres of water.

§2 Pressure is the rule
S9 Pressure rises by one atmosphere every 10 metres.
S10 At the bottom of the Challenger Deep, nearly 11 kilometres down, it passes 1,000 atmospheres.
S11 Steel deforms.
S12 Air spaces collapse.
S13 The animals that live there do not resist the pressure so much as ignore it.
S14 Their bodies are mostly water, which barely compresses, and their proteins are steadied by small molecules that keep them folding correctly.
S15 The cold is constant too: most deep water sits at about 4 °C all year.
S16 The result is an environment that barely changes from one century to the next.

§3 Light made by life
S17 Where the sun stops, animals take over the job.
S18 A survey off the California coast found that 76% of the animals observed in the water column could produce their own light.
S19 Bioluminescence is not a curiosity.
S20 It may be the most common form of communication on the planet.
S21 The light is used to hunt, to hide and to find a mate.
S22 Some squid squirt glowing clouds instead of ink.
S23 Some fish carry red searchlights that their prey cannot see.
S24 In the dark, light is language.

§4 Food falls from above
S25 Almost nothing grows in the deep.
S26 Most of its food arrives as marine snow: a slow drift of dead plankton, waste and dust from the sunlit water far above.
S27 A single flake can take weeks to reach the bottom.
S28 Only about 1% of what the surface produces ever lands on the seafloor.
S29 Whale falls can feed a community for decades.
S30 Hydrothermal vents, discovered in 1977, run on chemistry instead of sunlight.
S31 Cold seeps leak methane that microbes turn into food.
S32 At the vents, water heated by magma emerges at up to 400 °C.
S33 Bacteria feed on the minerals dissolved in it, and tube worms more than 2 metres long house the bacteria.
S34 Ultimately, it is a food chain that starts with rock, not sunlight.

§5 The map is mostly blank
S35 We have detailed maps of the Moon and of Mars.
S36 Of our own seafloor, only about a quarter has been mapped at high resolution.
S37 The rest is known from satellite estimates that blur anything smaller than a few kilometres.
S38 People first reached the deepest point of the ocean in 1960.
S39 Two men spent about 20 minutes on the bottom.
S40 Nobody went back for 52 years.

§6 Why it matters
S41 The deep sea stores heat and carbon on a scale that shapes the climate of the whole planet.
S42 It is also being considered for mining before it has been properly surveyed.
S43 That is why the next decade of exploration matters more than the last century.
S44 Put simply: we cannot protect what we have never seen.
