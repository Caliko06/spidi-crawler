A four-day week in municipal offices: results of a two-year trial

Abstract

In a two-year trial across eleven municipal offices, moving staff to a four-day week at full pay did not reduce the number of cases processed, lowered sick leave by a fifth and improved retention, but it lengthened waiting times for in-person services by about a day. We describe the design, the outcomes and the limits of what the trial can show.

Background

Interest in shorter working weeks has grown since several private firms reported gains in productivity and wellbeing. Evidence from public administration is thinner, because public offices cannot easily shift demand and must remain open to citizens for fixed hours. Debate on the topic is often heated and rarely informed by data. This trial asked whether a four-day week could be introduced in municipal offices without lowering the service citizens receive.

Methods

Eleven offices were randomly assigned to the four-day schedule, and fourteen comparable offices kept the five-day week as controls. Offices were stratified by size and by the share of work that is done at a counter. Staff on the four-day schedule worked 32 hours across four days with no change in pay, and each office chose which weekday to close.

The primary outcomes were the number of cases closed per employee per month, the days of sick leave, and the average waiting time for in-person services. Secondary outcomes included staff turnover, a survey of citizen satisfaction and a validated measure of burnout. Data were collected monthly for twenty-four months and analysed by an independent team that did not take part in the design. All offices consented to take part in the study.

Results

Cases closed per employee per month did not differ between the groups, with a mean of 61.2 in the four-day offices and 60.4 in the controls. Sick leave fell by 21 percent in the four-day offices relative to controls, with the largest reductions among staff over fifty. Turnover fell from 11.8 percent to 7.1 percent a year in the four-day offices, while the controls were unchanged. Waiting times for in-person services rose by an average of 0.9 days in the four-day offices, mainly in offices that closed on a Friday.

Citizen satisfaction scores were slightly lower in the four-day offices in the first six months and equal to the controls afterwards. Burnout scores improved by 0.4 standard deviations among staff on the four-day schedule. Detailed tables for each office are available on request. The effect on sick leave was stable across all eleven offices and did not depend on which day they closed.

Limitations

Offices knew which schedule they had, so neither staff nor managers were blind to the intervention, and some of the improvement in self-reported burnout may reflect expectation. Eleven offices is a small sample, and the confidence intervals for turnover are wide. The trial ran in a single country, with a particular labour law and a particular culture of public service. We did not measure the quality of decisions, only their number and speed. Further research is needed.

Discussion

The trial suggests that a four-day week can be introduced in municipal offices without losing output, with real gains in health and retention, but at a cost in access for people who need a counter. The waiting-time result is the one that matters most for policy, because it falls on citizens and not on staff. Offices that kept a rotating rota so the counter stayed open five days saw almost none of the increase, which points to a design fix rather than a fundamental limit. Whether the gains would hold beyond two years is unknown. We thank the staff of all participating offices for their patience.
