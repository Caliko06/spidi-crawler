Postmortem: the checkout outage of 14 March

This document was written by the payments team and reviewed by the incident commander and two engineers from the platform group. It follows our blameless format, so it names roles rather than people.

Summary

On 14 March a configuration change to the payment router sent most card traffic to a single processor that was already close to its limit, and checkout failed for 71 minutes for roughly 38 percent of customers. The change was safe in isolation but interacted with a retry setting that multiplied the load on that processor within seconds. We lost an estimated 412,000 euros in orders that did not come back, and no customer data was exposed. The incident was detected by a customer-facing alert rather than by our own monitoring.

Impact

Between 09:12 and 10:23 UTC, 38 percent of checkout attempts returned an error, concentrated in the three largest markets. The mobile apps failed more often than the web site, because they retry aggressively and made the throttling worse. About 9,100 customers contacted support during the incident, roughly four times the usual volume. Support staff handled them well under difficult conditions. Refunds for double charges were completed by the following Monday.

Timeline

At 09:04 a routine change moved the card router to a new weighting that favoured the cheapest processor. At 09:12 error rates on checkout began to climb. At 09:19 the on-call engineer was paged, but the page went to a channel that had been muted during an earlier incident and never unmuted. At 09:31 a customer-facing alert from the support tooling, not our own monitoring, told the incident commander that something was wrong.

At 09:40 the team rolled back the weighting change, but errors did not fall. At 09:58 an engineer noticed that the retry queue held more than two million pending requests. At 10:11 the team drained the queue and manually pinned traffic to the second processor, and errors fell within minutes. At 10:23 checkout returned to normal, and the incident was closed at 10:50.

Root cause

The root cause was an unbounded retry policy in the payment router, which resends a failed request immediately and without a limit. When the cheapest processor began throttling, every rejected request came straight back, so the load we sent grew faster than the processor's limit, and the throttling got worse instead of better. The weighting change was the trigger but not the cause, because any slowdown at that processor would have produced the same spiral. Rolling the change back did not help because the queue of retries kept the pressure up on its own.

Contributing factors

Our monitoring watched error rates per market but not retry queue depth, so the signal that mattered most was invisible. The page went to a muted channel, which cost us about twelve minutes of response time. A load test had been run for the new weighting, but it simulated a slow processor and not a throttling one. There were a few other minor issues, none of which changed the outcome. The runbook for the router had not been updated since the previous quarter.

What went well

The incident commander declared a major incident within minutes of the alert and kept a written log. The second processor was already integrated, so pinning traffic to it took one command. Communication with the support team was clear throughout.

What went badly

We found out from a customer-facing alert and not from our own monitoring. The rollback was the wrong first move, and it cost nearly half an hour. We were also unlucky that two of the three people who know the router were out that day.

Action items

  • Cap retries in the payment router at three attempts with exponential backoff, owned by the payments team and due by 28 March.
  • Add retry queue depth to the dashboards and alert on it, due by 31 March.
  • Audit every muted paging channel and require a quarterly review of the routing, due by 11 April.
  • Extend load tests to cover throttling as well as slowness, due by the end of April.
  • Update the router runbook.

The retry cap is the one that would have prevented this incident, and it ships first.
