The Dark Majority

Most of the living space on this planet has never seen sunlight. We have barely looked at it.

A planet of deep water

Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface, but the surface is the smallest part of the story. The average depth is close to 3,700 metres. Sunlight gives up long before that. By 200 metres there is too little of it for photosynthesis, and by 1,000 metres it is gone completely.

Everything below that line is the deep sea. It holds roughly 90% of the ocean's volume. In other words, the typical place to live on Earth is cold, dark and pressed under kilometres of water.

Pressure is the rule

Pressure rises by one atmosphere every 10 metres. At the bottom of the Challenger Deep, nearly 11 kilometres down, it passes 1,000 atmospheres. Steel deforms. Air spaces collapse.

The animals that live there do not resist the pressure so much as ignore it. Their bodies are mostly water, which barely compresses, and their proteins are steadied by small molecules that keep them folding correctly. The cold is constant too: most deep water sits at about 4 °C all year. The result is an environment that barely changes from one century to the next.

Light made by life

Where the sun stops, animals take over the job. A survey off the California coast found that 76% of the animals observed in the water column could produce their own light. Bioluminescence is not a curiosity. It may be the most common form of communication on the planet.

The light is used to hunt, to hide and to find a mate. Some squid squirt glowing clouds instead of ink. Some fish carry red searchlights that their prey cannot see. In the dark, light is language.

Food falls from above

Almost nothing grows in the deep. Most of its food arrives as marine snow: a slow drift of dead plankton, waste and dust from the sunlit water far above. A single flake can take weeks to reach the bottom. Only about 1% of what the surface produces ever lands on the seafloor.

  • Whale falls can feed a community for decades.
  • Hydrothermal vents, discovered in 1977, run on chemistry instead of sunlight.
  • Cold seeps leak methane that microbes turn into food.

At the vents, water heated by magma emerges at up to 400 °C. Bacteria feed on the minerals dissolved in it, and tube worms more than 2 metres long house the bacteria. Ultimately, it is a food chain that starts with rock, not sunlight.

The map is mostly blank

We have detailed maps of the Moon and of Mars. Of our own seafloor, only about a quarter has been mapped at high resolution. The rest is known from satellite estimates that blur anything smaller than a few kilometres.

People first reached the deepest point of the ocean in 1960. Two men spent about 20 minutes on the bottom. Nobody went back for 52 years.

Why it matters

The deep sea stores heat and carbon on a scale that shapes the climate of the whole planet. It is also being considered for mining before it has been properly surveyed. That is why the next decade of exploration matters more than the last century. Put simply: we cannot protect what we have never seen.
